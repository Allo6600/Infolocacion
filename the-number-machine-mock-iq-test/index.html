Matrix pattern completion

Raven's Progressive Matrices

A grid of abstract shapes with one cell missing, introduced by John C. Raven in 1938. People are surprised that a wordless puzzle is treated as a window into the mind, and many enjoy solving similar grids in puzzle books and apps.

Number series continuation

Number series puzzle

A row of numbers where the solver must find the next term, such as 2, 4, 8, 16. What surprises people is that many sequences fit more than one rule, so the 'correct' answer depends on what the test-maker intended.

Verbal analogies

Verbal analogy (A is to B as C is to ?)

A word puzzle asking solvers to complete a relationship, like 'bird is to nest as bee is to hive'. These items once appeared on American college entrance exams, and many people still enjoy them as quick party brain-teasers.

Odd one out

Odd one out

A list of items where one does not belong to the group. People are often surprised that a clever reason can be found for almost any choice, and it remains a favorite in children's activity books and pub quizzes.

Mental rotation task

Solvers judge whether two drawn 3D figures are the same object turned in space, a task studied by Roger Shepard and Jacqueline Metzler in 1971. Surprisingly, response time grows with the angle of rotation, as if the mind turns the image step by step.

Section B: The Machinery of Scoring 8

No photo
Time limits

Time limits (制限時間)

Many IQ subtests are timed, so speed quietly becomes part of what counts as intelligence. People are often surprised that a correct answer given slowly can earn fewer points, or none, on some tasks.

No photo
Raw score tally

Raw score (素点)

The raw score is simply the count of points earned on each subtest. It means little alone. It only becomes an IQ after being compared with other people's results, which surprises those who expect a direct measurement.

No photo
Norming sample

Norming sample (標準化サンプル)

Scores are interpreted against a reference group tested during standardization. Because average raw performance rose over decades, a pattern called the Flynn effect, tests must be renormed periodically. Your 'intelligence' therefore partly depends on when the test was normed.

Bell curve fitting

Normal distribution (正規分布)

Scores are converted so they follow a bell-shaped normal distribution. Many people assume the curve is discovered in nature. In practice, test makers build and scale the test to produce it, so the shape is partly a design choice.

Standard deviation of 15

Standard deviation of 15 (標準偏差15)

Most modern IQ tests, including the Wechsler scales, set one standard deviation at 15 points. About two-thirds of people therefore score between 85 and 115. The number 15 is a convention, not a natural constant.

Mean fixed at 100

Mean of 100 (平均100)

An IQ of 100 is defined as the average of the norming sample. Early IQ was a ratio of mental age to chronological age times 100. David Wechsler's deviation IQ kept 100 as the anchor, so the average is set by definition.

Percentile rank

Percentile rank (パーセンタイル順位)

A percentile rank shows the share of the norming group scoring at or below you. An IQ of 130 is roughly the 98th percentile. This reveals that IQ is fundamentally a ranking among people, not a measure of an amount.

Results from many different subtests are combined into one Full Scale IQ. Psychologists often stress that the separate index scores and their uneven profile can say more about a person than the single headline number that people remember.

Restriction of range
